Calderwick City Council · Parks & Green Infrastructure Report 26/04 August 2026

State of the Municipal Tree Canopy

Calderwick has lost a fifth of its tree canopy in sixteen years. The loss is not evenly spread, it is not mostly caused by development, and it is not being replaced by the planting programme designed to replace it.

Prepared for the Environment & Climate Scrutiny Committee · 22 pages · figures 1–6

Contents

    Section oneSummary of findings

    Canopy cover across the eleven wards of Calderwick stood at 17.9% in the spring 2025 survey, down from 21.4% in 2009.1 That is a relative decline of sixteen per cent over sixteen years, in a period during which the council planted more trees each year than in any comparable period in its history.

    HeadlineCanopy fell from 21.4% to 17.9% between 2009 and 2025, while annual planting rose by a factor of two and a half.

    Four findings follow from the survey, and they are uncomfortable in a particular order. First, the loss is concentrated: three wards account for more than half of the absolute canopy lost (Fig. 1). Second, those three wards are among the four most deprived in the city, so the canopy gap has widened along the same axis as every other environmental inequality here (Fig. 3). Third, the largest single driver is not development but disease — ash dieback has moved through a stock in which nearly one tree in five was ash (Fig. 4).2 Fourth, and most consequentially for policy, the replacement programme is failing quietly: planting numbers have risen every cohort since 2015 while five-year establishment survival has fallen from 71% to 48% (Fig. 5).

    Gross planting is not net canopy. A tree planted and lost within five years is an expense, not an asset, and the council currently reports the expense as the asset.

    Three of these four findings were visible in the 2019 survey and were not acted on. This report does not repeat the 2019 recommendations. It sets out what has changed since, what the 2025 data adds, and the three decisions that are now unavoidable.

    Section twoMethod, in brief

    Canopy cover is measured from leaf-on aerial imagery classified at 25 cm resolution and validated against a stratified ground sample of 480 points.3 Canopy cover here means the proportion of ground area beneath the vertical projection of tree crowns, including private gardens and rail and river corridors — not only council-owned stock, which is roughly a third of the total.

    Comparability2009 imagery was captured at a coarser resolution than 2019 and 2025. The re-analysis in Appendix A puts the resulting bias at under 0.4 percentage points and in the direction of understating loss.

    Full method, the re-cut of 2009 figures to current ward boundaries, and the four limitations that materially affect interpretation are in Appendix A. Readers who intend to quote a single number from this report should read that appendix first.

    Section threeWhere the canopy went

    Citywide canopy has declined at every survey since 2009, and the rate has accelerated (Fig. 2). The 2019–2025 interval lost 1.9 percentage points against 1.6 in the decade before it — a period half as long shedding more canopy.

    The adopted target of 22% by 2035 now requires adding more canopy in nine years than the city has lost in sixteen, starting from a stock that is younger, smaller-crowned and less established than the one it replaced. No scenario modelled in Appendix A reaches it.

    Section fourWho lost it

    Canopy in Calderwick tracks deprivation closely and has done for as long as it has been measured. The 2025 survey finds the relationship not merely intact but steeper (Fig. 3): the most deprived decile now sits at 7.5% mean cover against 28.6% in the least deprived, a gap that has widened by 2.3 percentage points since 2009.5

    Why it compoundsStreet trees in low-canopy wards are younger, smaller and more exposed to kerbside damage, so each loss removes proportionally more crown and each replacement returns less.

    This is the finding with the shortest route to a statutory duty. Eastfield's summer surface temperature runs 3.2°C above the city mean, and Eastfield contains two of the city's three care homes.

    Section fiveWhy: a monoculture meeting a pathogen

    Calderwick's canopy was, until recently, 18% ash by stem count — a legacy of post-war amenity planting that favoured a fast, cheap, locally abundant species (Fig. 5). Ground survey in 2025 found 62% of surveyed ash showing crown decline consistent with Hymenoscyphus fraxineus.8

    The arithmetic is not subtle. If the majority of declining ash is removed on safety grounds by 2032, as the arboricultural team's programme assumes, that alone removes roughly eleven percentage points of stems — before any replacement planting is counted, and concentrated in the wards where ash was planted most heavily, which are the wards already carrying the least canopy.

    Section sixWhat replacement actually delivers

    The council planted 3,100 trees in the 2023 cohort against 1,240 in 2015. Over the same period, establishment survival fell by 23 percentage points (Fig. 6). The 2021 cohort — the last with a complete five-year record — retained 54%.

    Unit cost per surviving tree has risen 79% in eight years. Roughly a third of that is input price inflation. The remainder is mortality: the programme is buying the same number of established trees for substantially more money, and reporting the gross figure to committee.

    Section sevenWhat follows

    Three decisions are now unavoidable, and only the first is primarily about money.

    One: report net, not gross. Committee papers should carry canopy area and five-year survival alongside trees planted. The current reporting line is not wrong, but it cannot detect the failure documented in Section six, and it did not.

    Two: fund three years of aftercare, or plant fewer trees. Watering and formative pruning through establishment is the intervention with evidence behind it. At current unit costs, 1,900 trees with three years of aftercare would return more established canopy than 3,100 without it, for a comparable budget.

    Three: weight planting by canopy deficit, not by ward request volume. The current allocation responds to requests, and requests come from wards that already have trees. That mechanism has produced the gap in Figure 3 and will widen it.

    Appendix AMethod and limitations

    Survey method

    Leaf-on imagery was captured between 15 June and 2 July 2025 at 25 cm ground sample distance and classified using a supervised model trained on 1,200 manually labelled tiles. Overall classification accuracy against the ground sample was 94.1%, with the residual error concentrated in distinguishing dense shrub from low crown.

    Four limitations that affect interpretation

    Resolution change. The 2009 survey used 50 cm imagery. Re-classifying the 2019 imagery at the coarser resolution suggests the older figure understates canopy by 0.3–0.4 percentage points, meaning true loss since 2009 is marginally greater than reported here, not less.

    Boundary change. Ward boundaries were redrawn in 2019. The 2009 ward figures in Figure 1 are re-cut to current boundaries by area-weighted apportionment, which is exact for canopy area and approximate for percentage cover. Beckside and Ingle Vale are the two wards most affected; treat differences under 0.5 points in those two as noise.

    Private land. Roughly two-thirds of canopy stands on land the council does not control. The policy levers in Section seven reach the other third. Any target expressed as total canopy is therefore partly a forecast of private behaviour.

    Surface, not air. Figure 4 uses land surface temperature, which is not the temperature a person experiences. It is a reliable indicator of relative thermal exposure between wards and an unreliable estimate of absolute heat risk.

    Scenario modelling

    Three scenarios were run to 2035: current programme, aftercare-funded, and aftercare-funded with deficit weighting. None reaches the adopted 22% target. The best case reaches 19.4% and closes the deprivation gap by 4.1 points, which is the outcome this report treats as the realistic objective.
